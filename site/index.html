The engine

Nothing here is faked for the camera

Built on Three.js and WebGL2 with no game engine underneath. The scene is computed from catalogues and physics every frame, which is why you can fly at it from any direction and it still holds up.

True scale, no cheating

Bodies are placed by ephemeris at their real separations and drawn at their real radii. The camera crosses thirteen orders of magnitude without a loading screen or a hidden change of units — the depth buffer is logarithmic so a rover and a gas giant can share one frame.

Surfaces from measurement

Terrain is spacecraft imagery over real elevation, streamed as a level-of-detail cubesphere. The Moon's relief comes from Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter data, so a crater you descend into is the crater that is actually there.

Atmospheres that scatter

Skies are integrated per-wavelength through Rayleigh and Mie scattering rather than tinted by hand. It is the same calculation that makes Earth's sunset red and, with a thinner atmosphere and more dust, makes Mars's sunset come out blue.

Stars with real physics

Radii are derived from luminosity and spectral type through Stefan–Boltzmann, so Betelgeuse arrives at 712 solar radii — 3.3 astronomical units, wide enough to swallow the orbit of Mars — and glows the colour its temperature says it should.

Light bent by gravity

Black holes are ray-marched through the Schwarzschild metric. The photon ring is not a texture or a sprite: it is light from behind the hole, bent around the front and arriving at the camera the long way.

Time you can drive

Wind the clock and the whole system moves on its real ephemeris — eclipses fall where they fall, moons shadow their planets, and a spacecraft is wherever JPL Horizons says it was on that date.

Get it

Run it on your desktop

The desktop build is the same renderer with the GPU gloves off — it can ask for the discrete adapter and keep the compositor off the main thread, which a browser tab cannot.

Where the numbers come from

Borrowed from people who measured it

Body imagery from Solar System Scope (CC-BY 4.0), derived from NASA elevation and imagery. Lunar elevation from the NASA Scientific Visualization Studio CGI Moon Kit, out of Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter data. Star data from the HYG database compiled by David Nash from the Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star and Gliese catalogues. Deep sky, constellation and asterism data from Olaf Frohn's d3-celestial. Milky Way panorama by Serge Brunier (ESO). Planetary elements from JPL; lunar theory from ELP2000 as presented in Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Spacecraft trajectories from the JPL Horizons system at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Landing site coordinates from LROC and HiRISE imagery as published by the Lunar and Planetary Institute and the USGS.
