From the creator

Why I built Cosminova

I spend a lot of nights at a telescope. You find Saturn, then a faint smudge that turns out to be a galaxy, and through the eyepiece both look painted on the same dark surface. Numbers like 1.4 billion kilometres or 2.5 million light-years are easy to repeat and impossible to feel.

I wanted one place where those distances are real and continuous: stand on the ground under the actual sky, then keep pulling back — past the Moon, the planets, the nearest stars, the Milky Way — without a single cut. The emptiness between things is the part I most wanted to see, so nothing here is moved closer to make it fit.

— Grant Su

The engine

Measured where it can be, generated where it can't

Built on Three.js and WebGL2 with no game engine underneath. Orbits, surfaces, star positions and galaxies come from catalogues wherever someone has measured them. Beyond that, stars, planets, black holes and galaxies are generated by rules taken from the real populations, and the info panel marks each one as generated. Either way, nothing is moved closer or scaled up to look better on camera.

True scale, no cheating

Bodies are placed by ephemeris at their real separations and drawn at their real radii. The camera crosses thirteen orders of magnitude without a loading screen or a hidden change of units — the depth buffer is logarithmic so a rover and a gas giant can share one frame.

Surfaces from measurement

Terrain is spacecraft imagery over real elevation, streamed as a level-of-detail cubesphere. The Moon's relief comes from Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter data, so a crater you descend into is the crater that is actually there. The shot above is at a grazing sun, because relief is invisible at noon.

Atmospheres that scatter

Skies are integrated per-wavelength through Rayleigh and Mie scattering rather than tinted by hand. It is the same calculation that makes Earth's sunset red and, with a thinner atmosphere and more dust, makes the glow around the Martian sun come out blue.

Stars with real physics

Radii come from luminosity and spectral type through Stefan–Boltzmann, and colour from temperature — which is why Sirius above is blue-white rather than yellow. The same arithmetic puts Betelgeuse at 712 solar radii: 3.3 astronomical units, wide enough to swallow the orbit of Mars.

Light bent by gravity

Black holes are ray-marched through the Schwarzschild metric. The photon ring is not a texture or a sprite: it is light from behind the hole, bent around the front and arriving at the camera the long way. The disc appears above and below the shadow at once because you are seeing both its faces.

Spacecraft, where they were

A hundred and nineteen craft, landers, rovers and landing sites, each on its own trajectory rather than parked at a decorative offset. You can ride along and look out from the craft itself — above is Hubble, at its real size, over the ground it was passing.

Time you can drive

Wind the clock and the whole system moves on its real ephemeris — eclipses fall where they fall, moons shadow their planets, and a spacecraft is wherever JPL Horizons says it was on that date.

Where the numbers come from

Borrowed from people who measured it

Body imagery from Solar System Scope (CC-BY 4.0), derived from NASA elevation and imagery. Lunar elevation from the NASA Scientific Visualization Studio CGI Moon Kit, out of Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter data. Star data from the HYG database compiled by David Nash from the Hipparcos, Yale Bright Star and Gliese catalogues. Deep sky, constellation and asterism data from Olaf Frohn's d3-celestial. Milky Way panorama by Serge Brunier (ESO). Planetary elements from JPL; lunar theory from ELP2000 as presented in Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Spacecraft trajectories from the JPL Horizons system at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Landing site coordinates from LROC and HiRISE imagery as published by the Lunar and Planetary Institute and the USGS. Faint stars from ESA's Gaia DR3; exoplanet systems from the NASA Exoplanet Archive; galaxies from Cosmicflows-3, RC3 and HyperLEDA.

What is not measured. Nobody has photographed an exoplanet's surface, so every exoplanet's face is imagined from its size, temperature and orbit. Past the catalogues, the stars, planets, moons, black holes and galaxies you find are generated from a seed, so the same place always looks the same — but none of them was observed.
